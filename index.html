The ocean keeps us alive.

It produces much of the oxygen we breathe, absorbs heat and CO₂, and feeds billions of people. Getij explains what threatens it, what has been proven to work, and what you can do today.

Why the ocean matters

50%

of the oxygen on Earth is estimated to come from the ocean, mostly from phytoplankton.

90%

of the extra heat from climate change since 1970 has been absorbed by the ocean.

¼

of the CO₂ that people emit is taken up by seawater every year.

3.3 bn

people get at least a fifth of their animal protein from fish and seafood.

The ocean is the largest habitat on Earth and the least explored. It regulates our climate, provides food and jobs, and protects coastlines from storms. Coral reefs, seagrass meadows and mangroves break the waves and serve as nurseries for fish.

These systems are under pressure from what happens on land and at sea: too much fishing, too much plastic, too much CO₂. The good news is that the ocean often recovers surprisingly fast once we take the pressure off. That is what this site is about.

Five threats

37.7%of fish stocks are overfished
Overfishing

We catch fish faster than they can reproduce

In 1974 about 10% of monitored marine fish stocks were overfished; today it is more than a third. Large fleets, subsidised for fuel and vessels, fish far outside their own waters. Illegal and unreported fishing makes the problem worse.

Source: FAO, The State of World Fisheries and Aquaculture 2024 (2021 data)

11 Mtof plastic enters the sea each year
Plastic

A garbage truck full every minute

An estimated 11 million tonnes of plastic end up in the ocean every year, and without action that will nearly triple by 2040. Animals get entangled or swallow it. Plastic breaks down into microplastics, which are now found from the sea surface to the deepest trenches.

Source: The Pew Charitable Trusts & SYSTEMIQ, Breaking the Plastic Wave (2020)

+30%more acidic since the Industrial Revolution
Warming & acidification

Warmer, more acidic and lower in oxygen

Dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid. The pH of surface water has dropped from about 8.2 to 8.1. That sounds small, but the scale is logarithmic: the water is roughly 30% more acidic. Shellfish, corals and calcifying algae struggle to build their skeletons. Warmer water also holds less oxygen, and marine heatwaves are becoming more frequent.

Source: IPCC, Special Report on the Ocean and Cryosphere (2019); NOAA

84%of reef area hit by bleaching-level heat
Coral bleaching

The largest bleaching event ever recorded

Reefs cover less than 1% of the seafloor, yet about a quarter of all marine species depend on them. When water gets too warm, corals expel the algae that feed them and give them colour. If the heat lasts too long, they die. The fourth global bleaching event has been under way since 2023; by early 2025, 84% of the world's reef area had been exposed to bleaching-level heat stress.

Source: NOAA Coral Reef Watch & International Coral Reef Initiative (April 2025)

Bycatch & sharks

The catch nobody wanted

An estimated 100 million sharks are killed every year, for fins and meat and as bycatch. Sharks mature late and have few young, so populations recover slowly. More than a third of all shark and ray species are threatened with extinction. Sea turtles, albatrosses and dolphins also die in nets and on longlines. Only a handful of vaquitas, a porpoise from the Gulf of California, remain.

Sources: Pacoureau et al., Nature (2021); Worm et al., Marine Policy (2013); Dulvy et al., Current Biology (2021)

What has been proven to work

Protected areas · 30x30

Leave the sea alone and it comes back

In Cabo Pulmo, Mexico, local fishers chose to stop fishing in 1995. Ten years later the amount of fish had more than quintupled, and sharks and groupers had returned. Under the UN biodiversity agreement of 2022, 30% of the ocean should be protected by 2030. We are still far from that:

≈2.8% fully or highly protected ≈8.4% protected on paper target: 30% by 2030

Sources: Aburto-Oropeza et al., PLoS ONE (2011); Protected Planet / MPAtlas (2025)

High seas
2/3

A treaty for the open ocean

Almost two thirds of the ocean lies outside any country's waters. The UN High Seas Treaty (BBNJ) makes it possible to create protected areas there. It was adopted in 2023 and entered into force on 17 January 2026.

Subsidies

Stop paying for overfishing

Governments spend tens of billions of dollars a year on fisheries subsidies, much of which makes fleets bigger. Since September 2025, a WTO agreement has banned subsidies for illegal fishing and for fishing overfished stocks.

Smarter fishing

Less bycatch with simple changes

Turtle excluder devices in shrimp nets, circle hooks instead of J-hooks, acoustic pingers to warn off porpoises, and bird-scaring lines on longlines. They cost little and cut bycatch substantially.

Plastic at the source

Turn off the tap before you mop

Clean-ups help locally, but the biggest gains come from less single-use plastic, deposit return schemes and better waste collection. Since 2022, countries have been negotiating a binding UN plastics treaty.

Back to the coastNorth Sea & Wadden Sea

Ocean protection starts close to home

The North Sea is one of the busiest and most heavily fished seas in the world. Even so, it is home to harbour porpoises, seals, rays and even sharks, such as the small-spotted catshark and the starry smooth-hound. The Wadden Sea is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and a refuelling stop for millions of migratory birds.

The egg cases of sharks and rays wash up on the beach, and you can report them. And where bottom-trawling has stopped, such as inside offshore wind farms, the seabed gets a chance to recover.
